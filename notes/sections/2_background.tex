\section{Background}


\textbf{A. Coordination Systems} \\
Recent advances in multi-agent systems have demonstrated the potential application in fleets of mobile robots to automate warehouse operations, including item picking, sorting, and transportation \cite{agaskar2025deepfleet}. Effective coordination among robots is crucial to ensure efficient task allocation, collision avoidance, and overall system performance.

We build on queueing theory for edge and node delays, specifically using Kingman's approximation for G/G/1 queues, age-of-information (AoI) bounds for gossip-based distributed shared memory, and standard centralized batch allocation models.

\textbf{B. Stigmergy and Distributed Coordination} \\
Stigmergy, a mechanism of indirect coordination through the environment, has been widely studied in swarm robotics \cite{garnier2007biological, brambilla2013swarm}. In warehouse settings, stigmergic approaches can enable robots to share information about task status and environmental changes without centralized control \cite{payton2001pheromone, labella2004division}. This can lead to more robust and scalable systems, as robots can adapt to dynamic conditions and locally optimize their behavior. A successful implementation requires a well-designed shared memory and communication architecture.

\textbf{C. Inference Systems for Embodied Robotic Agents} \\
Besides coordination, mobile robots often rely on onboard AI models for perception, navigation, and decision-making. Recent work has explored the deployment of deep learning models on resource-constrained devices, enabling real-time inference for tasks such as object detection, semantic segmentation, and path planning \cite{redmon2018yolov3, he2017mask}. Vision language Action Models (VLMs) have shown promise in enabling robots to understand and execute complex instructions by grounding language in visual perception \cite{shah2023vlm, singh2022flamingo}. However, deploying these models on mobile robots presents challenges related to computational efficiency, latency, and energy consumption.
Device-aware edge inference SDKs, such as Nexa, provide efficient and portable execution of AI models on heterogeneous hardware. However, these SDKs do not address multi-agent task dissemination, claiming, or AoI-bounded coordination. Our contribution is a network-centric orchestration approach: a trace and signal dissemination layer combined with a local claiming policy that reduces task propagation time and increases the stability region compared to centralized and hybrid baselines, independent of the specific per-device model runtime.

We validate this approach across multiple runners (TensorRT, ORT, Nexa) to demonstrate that the results are determined by the coordination architecture rather than the SDK implementation.
